# Window 03 — 160–141 BCE: A Law Against Counterfeit Gold Is Not a Law Against Alchemy

**Status:** SOURCED SECONDARY REVIEW + ONE TRANSLATED PRIMARY-TEXT PASS; OPEN FOR PHILOLOGICAL COLLATION  
**Date span:** exactly twenty years, 160–141 BCE, inclusive.  
**Independent geographies:** Ptolemaic Egypt / Western Han China. **No exchange, transmission, or personal lineage between them has been established.**  
**Founding question:** How do outside pressures shape the recognition, restriction, technical capacity, and language of a specialized station?  
**Core finding:** In China a 144 BCE law protects the money/gold domain from forgery, but does **not** explicitly suppress alchemical elixir practices. Egypt undergoes a royal succession in 145 BCE; no alchemical station is documented as directly transformed by it.

## 01 — THERE: historical conditions

### Egypt (Greco-Egyptian antecedents)
- **160–145 BCE:** Ptolemy VI Philometor and Cleopatra II hold royal authority, amid continued Ptolemaic priestly and administrative institutions. [W03-E01–E03]
- **145 BCE:** Ptolemy VI dies in a Syrian conflict. Ptolemy VIII assumes the Egyptian throne and marries Cleopatra II. Royal legitimacy and potential patronage are reorganized. [W03-E01–E03]
- **142–141 BCE:** An archaeological/epigraphic study associates inscriptions on the temple of Edfu's naos exterior with Ptolemy VIII and its consecration, but the details require verification against the edition and inscriptions. [W03-E04]
- **Boundary:** all are political, royal, ritual, material or epigraphic histories. **None independently identifies a practicing alchemist or an alchemical secrecy regime** for this window.

### China (parallel and separate)
- **Emperor Jing (reigned 156–141 BCE):** laws surrounding privately minted coins and counterfeit gold are part of government oversight of valuable materials and exchange. [W03-C01–C03]
- **144 BCE:** the *Hanshu*, compiled much later, dates a law in the sixth middle year to punishment for illicit coinage and counterfeit gold. Its wording does not explicitly mention alchemists, elixirs, or a rite of transformation. [W03-C01]
- Earlier scholarship (especially Dubs 1947) inferred that this decree suppressed alchemy; Pregadio's source-critical analysis rejects that inference as conflating metal forgery with ritual and elixir practice. [W03-C02–C03]

## 02 — Material technique, apparatus and demonstrable practice
**Egypt:** metal coinage, textile manufacture, cult images, inscribed architectural material and craft work are antecedent conditions. No securely dated Egyptian alchemical procedure, laboratory notebook or specialized apparatus has been established in this specific interval.

**China:** illegal monetary fabrication and counterfeit precious-metal objects are the stated domain of the 144 BCE law. A law naming fabrication does not furnish an actual recipe or prove successful transmutation. [W03-C01–C02]

**Broad, unassigned:** The lost *Hongbao yuanbi shu* (Arts from the Garden of Secrets of the Great Treasure), associated by scholarship with the mid-second century BCE, is a potential early *external alchemy* textual lead; its text does not survive, and the date cannot be securely narrowed to 160–141 BCE. Later references are not surviving copies from this window. [W03-C02, W03-C04]

## 03 — Language, technique and possible allegory
### The central philological distinction
*Hanshu* 5, Emperor Jing annals, has **「定鑄錢偽黃金棄市律」** (*ding zhu qian wei huang jin qi shi lü*) in the December entry for the sixth middle year. The clause establishes a publicly enforced penalty relating to unauthorized minting and fraudulent gold. [W03-C01]

**Important:** The term **偽** (*wei*, false/counterfeit) belongs to a legal concern with forgery; it is **not proof of a disguised alchemical ingredient**. Similarly, 金 (*jin*, metal/gold) may vary by context; do not identify its referent without a close reading. Compare the later commentators Ying Shao, Meng Kang and Yan Shigu as separate, much later acts of interpretation. [W03-C01, W03-C03]

### Edfu and temple language
Hieroglyphic temple inscriptions and royal titulary display religious and political legitimacy. They are not automatically encrypted versions of chemical techniques.

### Early Chinese esoteric language
Legends of the Yellow Emperor, elixirs, spirits, burners/stoves, immortality, and hidden books occur in the longer Chinese alchemical record, but texts bearing those motifs need their own composition dates and witnesses. **Do not place an exact allegorical utterance into 160–141 BCE based on a broad 'mid-second-century' attribution.** [W03-C02, W03-C04]

## 04 — Secrecy, access, qualification, danger
| Hypothesis | What the sources support in 160–141 BCE | Verdict for an Alchemist's secrecy |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional monopoly | Jing's edict polices counterfeit coinage/gold; Egypt's institutions recognize royal/temple standing | **NOT PROVEN** as an alchemical monopoly |
| Fear of persecution | Violations of Jing's monetary law could lead to execution; no proof of punishment for alchemical elixirs as such | **NOT PROVEN** for alchemists |
| Economic value | Gold and monetary trust carry strong state interest | **DOCUMENTED CONCERN** in legal domain; motive concerning alchemy unproven |
| Skilled initiation / capacity | Metalworking, forgery, ritual and medicine demand knowledge, but no dated apprenticeship test of the Alchemist has been found | **NO SECURE EVIDENCE** |
| Physical/spiritual danger | Monetary fraud threatened social order; no direct law tying restricted elixir access to toxicity or metaphysical danger | **NO SECURE EVIDENCE** |
| Prestige / claimed exceptional authority | Royal ritual, inscriptions and later secret-book claims provide comparative interpretive possibilities | **INTERPRETATION; NOT TRACED** to a dated practitioner |
| Apparent secrecy due to lost witnesses | *Hongbao yuanbi shu* is lost, known through later reports | **TEXTUAL LOSS DOCUMENTED**, deliberate concealment not demonstrated |

**Critical distinction:** an outsider's criminalized *counterfeit activity* ≠ an alchemist's specialized *ritual/technical vocation*. The prohibition does not show that only palace alchemists could practice under royal permission.

## 05 — Perception
- **Chinese legal voice:** fabrication of currency or counterfeit gold is a punishable offense; state authority makes this distinction public. [W03-C01]
- **Later interpretive voice:** a historian or commentator may turn a statement about forged metal into a story about forbidden alchemy; this is a *subsequent perception*, not the law itself. [W03-C02–C03]
- **Egyptian royal/ritual voice:** dynastic succession and cultic monuments show that royal recognition could change through political pressure. No source is cited here where an alchemical master specifically says authority was lost.

## 06 — Station (office, competence, recognition)
**ATTESTED:** Han sovereign/legal authority and the domain of illicit monetary manufacture; named Ptolemaic royal stations and priestly/ruling institutions.

**NOT ATTESTED in this 20-year window by a closely dated primary witness:** a named Greek-Egyptian alchemist; an authorized Egyptian alchemical office; a Han practitioner explicitly granted exclusive lawful right to make elixirs because of the 144 edict; a standardized credential for an alchemical trade.

**Terminology note:** the Han *fangshi* (specialist/master of methods) is a later contextually significant category, not automatically an interchangeable translation of the modern word *alchemist*. The first secure well-known court episode with Li Shaojun is around **133 BCE**, which belongs to **140–121 BCE** (Window 04). [W03-C04]

## 07 — People and legitimate lineage
**Egypt:** Ptolemy VI, Ptolemy VIII, Cleopatra II, priestly officials; a change in royal rule is not a demonstrated succession of alchemical practitioners. [W03-E01–E03]

**China:** Emperor Jing (decree); later historians and commentators interpreting his law (Ban Gu, Ying Shao and subsequent writers). Their writings form a **lineage of interpretation of a law**, not a teacher–student lineage of goldmakers. [W03-C01–C03]

**Beyond boundary:** Li Shaojun circa 133 BCE is deliberately reserved for next window; the lost *Hongbao yuanbi shu* has no secure surviving contemporaneous copy. [W03-C02, W03-C04]

## 09 — HERE: what changes and what remains
**Warranted:**
1. We can distinguish a documented **external legal pressure** around money and precious-metal forgery in Han China from a *hypothetical* persecution of alchemists.
2. A source-critical genealogy can distinguish the **historical event** (144 law) from the **later interpretation** ('law against alchemy').
3. Ptolemaic royal succession in 145 BCE creates a documented shift of sovereign authority, but no observable shift of a specialized alchemical station.
4. Both ancient societies show institutions controlling some forms of authorized action; they do not establish the same profession or a transmission between them.

**Not warranted:** an original secret alchemical brotherhood, a government monopoly on all alchemical activity, a cause of esoteric vocabulary, a mass persecution of alchemists, or a single founding master.

**Remainder:** What would a contemporary trained practitioner have called the practice, what did their patron pay for, and how did competence relate to recognition? To answer requires closer surviving technical texts than this window presently provides.

## 10 — THE OUTSIDE: pressure → station response
| Vector | Han China | Ptolemaic Egypt |
|---|---|---|
| Outside force | Government regulation of currency and fraudulent precious metals | Royal dynastic turnover and cultic patronage |
| Event | 144 BCE counterfeit coinage/gold law | 145 BCE succession of Ptolemy VIII |
| Station demonstrably pressured | Illicit makers of coinage/counterfeit gold, and broader currency users | Political royal station; temple relations |
| Alchemical specialist demonstrably changed? | **NO SECURE EVIDENCE** | **NO SECURE EVIDENCE** |
| Language of pressure | Legal term for counterfeiting; penalty | Kingly title, cultic legitimacy, inscription |
| Retained or suppressed knowledge | Actual effect on craft knowledge undetermined | Actual effect on craft knowledge undetermined |
| Distinct HERE | State criminal boundary around money/gold forgery | New bearer of existing royal station |
| Next test | Later Li Shaojun court episode: skill, patronage, ritual, transmutation | Find close-dated workshop and practitioner testimony |

### The strongest finding
**Regulation of material value is not the same as regulation of transformative knowledge.** Confusing them invents a story of secrecy and persecution that the primary wording cannot bear.
