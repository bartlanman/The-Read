# Window 28 — Knowledge Protected, Prediction Criminalized: Language, Technique, Access and Secrecy (341–360 CE)

**Evidence standard:** distinct ancient law and material witnesses, with text witnesses transmitted in the **438 Theodosian Code** rather than original-period preserved tablets. [Window 28](../02_CHRONOLOGY_20_YEAR_WINDOWS/CE_0341-0360_CE__WINDOW.md) · [source register](../08_SOURCE_REGISTER/CE_0341-0360__SOURCE_REGISTER.md). No original document here identifies the Greek `chēmeutēs` or authorizes chrysopoeia.

## A. What sorts of knowledge count? 344 versus 357

**344 — official valuation of skilled measurement and instruction.** **CTh XIII.4.3**, as transmitted, addresses `mechanicos et geometras et architectos`, with concrete attention to `divisiones` (divisions), `incisiones` (parts/cuts), `mensurae` (measurements), and `aquarum ... ductus` (water conduits). It urges `docendi adque discendi` (teaching and learning) and extends `inmunitates` (exemptions) to those qualified to teach. **One need not demonstrate the success of each construction project for this law to show that imperial authority wished to support instruction in practical engineering.** It does not name chemical transmutation or any particular teacher.

**357 — the politically prohibited inquiry into the future.** **CTh IX.16.4**, as transmitted, says `Nemo haruspicem consulat aut mathematicum, nemo hariolum` (“no one shall consult an entrail-diviner, an astrologer, a soothsayer”), then adds `Chaldaei ac magi`, `malefici`, and `divinandi curiositas` to the denunciation and demands a severe penalty. **Here `mathematicus` means astrologer in the specific legislative list**, not the **`geometra`** whose technical measurements were valued in **344**. A historian who translated both as “mathematician” would manufacture a legal contradiction that is not securely present.

**358 — political proximity increases danger.** **CTh IX.16.6** attacks court `magi`, diviners, dream interpreters and `mathematici` with court-specific coercive penalties. The named audience is crucial: it is a regulation of political/ritual consultation linked to imperial safety, not an empirical prohibition on reconstructing dye or metal-coloring recipes.

**Important:** the code's chapter is titled `De maleficis et mathematicis et ceteris similibus`; chapter heading reflects the codification's **legal taxonomy**, which is not a licensed self-title of every actor condemned.

## B. The 'elementa' trap in 357

**CTh IX.16.5** alleges people involved in `magicis artibus` dare `elementa turbare` and harm the lives of innocents while calling upon the dead (`manibus accitis`). These are accusatory acts the law **claims** could be performed. Do **not** import modern chemistry's “chemical elements” into the plural `elementa` here: it belongs to a language of disturbing the natural order/weather or matter, accompanying harmful incantation, not an account of assaying gold produced from copper.

A threatening penalty confirms **state perceptions/claims** about danger and eligibility to consult; it does **not** independently prove a named person performed such magic, nor that the ruling created a persecution of a chemical craft guild. Keep `magus`, `haruspex`, `mathematicus` (astrologer), `geometra` (geometer), `aurifex` (goldworker) and later Greek `chēmeutēs` separate unless a witness explicitly connects one identified person's actual activities.

## C. A sacred place might be regulated in contrasting ways

- **341 CTh XVI.10.2:** `Cesset superstitio, sacrificiorum aboleatur insania` targets **sacrifices**; subscription gives **341 by consular dating**, not secure day/month. Says nothing about forbidding metallurgy.
- **XVI.10.3:** temples outside city walls linked to public games should remain `intactae incorruptaeque`. Consular/date transmitted as **1 Nov 346**, scholarly emendation **342**. It protects certain buildings despite the rhetoric against superstition.
- **XVI.10.4:** temple closure/sacrifice penalty, standard transmitted date **1 Dec 346**, possible **354** correction. **Date CONTESTED**; a closed-temple prescription is not proof a named Egyptian alchemical school was evicted.
- **353 XVI.10.5:** nocturnal sacrifice restriction claims Magnentius had permitted it, but the Magnentian grant itself is not a separately original document in this case.
- **356 XVI.10.6:** prescribed capital punishment for proven participation in sacrifices or reverence for statues. We must distinguish **legal risk** from demonstrated enforcement or actual victims.

A model of “all traditional temples closed in 346” would ignore the conservation directive, source chronology problems and local legal implementation. Both laws are preserved through a **later selected code**; modern historians must not infer uniform effects from a normative sentence.

## D. Actual materials and procedures: what can be dated here?

**Original period comparator:** British Museum **B.13591** was minted at **Alexandria 351–355**, a **1.41g / 18mm copper-alloy nummus**, with **Constantius Gallus** portrait and mounted fallen-enemy reverse. The object proves physical handling and minting of copper alloy and portrayal of public authority. It does **not** contain a reagent sequence, proof of elemental transmutation, named mintworker or training lineage.

**Broader recipe horizon:** Greek **Leiden X and Stockholm** papyri contain formulations involving metals, colors, imitative products and dyeing, but copied within **broad late-third/early-fourth-century horizons**. They cannot be used to posit a specifically documented **347 laboratory reaction** or to infer that a `magus` prohibited in 357 possessed a particular recipe.

**Named authorship:** **Zosimos of Panopolis**, broadly c.300, is a recognized alchemical commentary writer, mainly through much later transmitted Greek manuscripts. No original **341–360** literary autograph, dated pupil contract, court allegation or persecution record is demonstrated. Themistius's **367** reference to metallic transmutation lies in the **next 361–380 window** and is explicitly excluded here.

## F. THERE → ENCOUNTER → HERE, with a necessary remainder

**THERE:** in the late Roman empire, practitioners of numerate construction, the engineering of aqueducts and physical operations with metals can be distinguished from ritual consultants who claim ability to anticipate future outcomes. The law and coin witnesses each have different probative power.

**ENCOUNTER:** 344 law creates a **normative legal shelter** for technical teachers; 357 law places **normative penalties** upon divination. A person might in principle combine these roles, but **no identified 341–360 practitioner demonstrably occupies both**. Coin makes a material mark; edict makes a legal category; a later compilation preserves the text.

**HERE:** one political regime's apparent contradiction dissolves when the **kind of knowledge** and **social threat perception** are kept visible. Public mathematical measurement in engineering is valued; private predictive influence over rulers is criminalized. **This is a documented sorting of stations by claimed operations, social audience and function**, not the chronological creation or disappearance of Alchemists.

**THE MOST PROFOUND RESULT:** The social fate of a specialized practitioner is determined not merely by whether their knowledge involves unusual natural phenomena but by **what institution they work through, what they claim, and which audience receives their claim**. In this interval, there is no direct bridge demonstrating that anyone entitled to interpret chemical metal-coloring was classified either as protected technical teacher or illegal diviner.

**REMAINDER:** actual law enforcement and the people punished, exact temple-law chronology, independent evidence of state-supported `geometra` teaching classes, any chemical operation linked to 357 magic laws, and primary alchemical dated source/teacher.
