# Window 21 — One Papyrus Makes Citizens and Expels Migrants

**Original object:** P.Giss.40, a Greek **c.215 CE** papyrus with (1) the **212/213 citizenship grant**, badly damaged, and (2) a later **c.215 instruction about Egyptian migrants' presence in Alexandria**, with trade-specific exemptions. Modern scholars argue over portions of the papyrus's restorations and even how many separate **edicts** it contains.

## 1. Authority can broaden legal belonging while narrowing local access
The `Constitutio Antoniniana` enfranchises nearly all **free** residents of Rome's empire. But an administrator can subsequently order certain **Egyptian rural immigrants** to leave Alexandria, and explicitly exempt **pig traders, river boatmen, and reed suppliers** useful to the city.

**Do not flatten:** grant of `civitas` ≠ unrestricted right to reside in any particular urban jurisdiction; residence/access ≠ professional eligibility; professional usefulness ≠ possession of a secret chemical formula.

**Ethical/historical limit:** These are categories used by imperial officials, including derogatory distinctions, and must be reported as **what the authority said**, not an endorsement or complete statement of migrants' actual rights and lives.

## 2. A damaged legal text can create false precision
The left column is incomplete. Scholars use supplied/restored Greek text to discuss exceptions such as `dediticii`. There are **competing interpretations** of that exception, and Peter van Minnen challenges whether all separate texts on the papyrus should count as three edicts. **A known legal idea does not grant certainty to missing letters.**

## 3. The 203 inscription that changed after 211
On the real Arch of Septimius Severus, Geta's name was in the 203 dedication. Caracalla killed him in **December 211** and imperial instructions caused Geta's name to be removed. **The surface and sockets retain traces of the erased line.**

Unlike Domitian's distant unreadable hieroglyphic cartouches that survived in Benevento (previous window 15), **Geta's prominent Latin inscription was directly targeted**. The comparison matters: erasure can target **legibility, location, office and propaganda**, yet the material operation creates its own remaining evidence.

## 4. Clement's 42 books — knowledge distributed across offices
`Stromata` VI.4, broadly composed around **198–203 CE**, says Egyptian temples assigned **36 Hermetic books** to priestly learning and **six medical ones** to `pastophoroi`. The categories cover bodies, disease, instruments, drugs, eye conditions and women's health. This is a **near-period writer's report**, not an actual recovered library or a named 210 CE curriculum from an original papyrus.

**Important distinction:** `Hermes` here is **textual/religious authority**. `Pastophoros` is described priestly office and medical learning, not an identifiable formal occupation of Alchemist. The account provides a **candidate structure for studying distributed expertise**, not proof of a named second-century chemical master.

## 5. Real persecution cannot fill an unrelated historical gap
Around 203 Christian martyrs suffered under Severan rule; a purported uniform 202 ban on conversion is **historiographically disputed**. Dio reports Caracalla stripped privileges of Aristotelian professors in Alexandria later. **Neither evidence group identifies a goldmaking Alchemist as victim or source of deliberately allegorical recipes.**
