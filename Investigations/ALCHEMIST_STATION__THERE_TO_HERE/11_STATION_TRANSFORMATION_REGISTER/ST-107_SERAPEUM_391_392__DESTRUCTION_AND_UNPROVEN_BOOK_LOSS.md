# ST-107 — The Serapeum ca.391/392: Real Sanctuary Loss Is Not Proven Alchemical Knowledge Loss

**Scope:** documented/contested end of a major Alexandrian Serapis cult institution, **not an authenticated alchemy book purge**. **Historical event:** ca.**391 CE**, alternatively **392**; **earliest narrative Rufinus ca.402**, followed by Eunapius late-fourth/early-fifth and **Socrates/Sozomen ca.440s**; independent site archaeology. **Grade:** SOURCE-SUPPORTED historical sanctuary destruction, narrative details and books **CONTESTED**. [Window 30](../02_CHRONOLOGY_20_YEAR_WINDOWS/CE_0381-0400_CE__WINDOW.md).

## THERE: surrounding conditions and existing station
The Serapeum was a prominent actual Alexandrian temple complex of **Serapis**, with known religious setting and monumental structure. Egyptian officials received 391 anti-sacrifice laws. Theophilus bishop of Alexandria figures in later narratives of conflict.

## CAPACITY: what the actor demonstrably could do
The sanctuary supported religious observance and had real architectural and material presence. Archaeology/narratives do not demonstrate a named **goldmaker**, specific chemical training program, or any elemental transformation laboratory.

## PERCEPTION: what contemporaries believed or claimed
Rufinus and later Christian writers narrate victory over idols and destruction; Eunapius presents strongly hostile alternative. **Literary agendas and temporal distances must remain explicit**, not harmonized into neutral first-person eyewitness action.

## AUTHORITY: who recognized, employed, patronized or challenged the actor
Imperial law changed legal standing of sacrifice in Egypt in 391. The sanctuary's subsequent destruction shows a real institution lost its center, but the **391 statute itself does not name the Serapeum** as demolition target or list chemical knowledge to confiscate.

## SECRECY: institution / fear / economic value / competence / danger / prestige / loss
A destroyed building does not automatically mean its library was active until 391, that all books burned, or that alchemical texts specifically were targeted. Competing scholarship examines whether a Serapeum library survived this late; **inventory and survival unknown**. No proven “lost alchemical key” directly caused by destruction.

## LANGUAGE: exact terms, translation, technical idioms, pseudonyms, allegory, ambiguity
`Serapis` and Serapeum refer to a god/sanctuary, not a professional title “Alchemist.” The later dialogue's “priest of Serapis” **cannot be matched to a person at destruction** from its title alone.

## THE OUTSIDE: external pressure and the date it entered
391 legal pressure, religious conflict and church leadership form a credible external context for temple's demise around **391/392**. Exact imperial instruction/political chain still debated.

## RESPONSE: changes, resistance, accommodation, continuity, or unverified
Temple destruction historically supported; reactions reported in competing Christian/pagan narratives. **No alchemist's actual exile, recipe coding, lost apparatus or student migration established**.

## TEXTS MANIFESTED: work, composition date, copy date, passage and authority claim
**Rufinus *Historia Ecclesiastica* XI.22–23 c.402**; **Eunapius *Lives of the Philosophers* late fourth/early fifth**; **Socrates HE V.16 c.440**; **Sozomen HE VII.15 440s**; [Sabottka *Das Serapeum in Alexandria* (2008)](https://www.cealex.org/publication/das-serapeum/). Narratives **not independent 391 written autographs**.

## HERE: what becomes newly warranted
A prominent actual institution loses its physical sanctuary, whereas the fate of supposed **alchemical knowledge** must be separately established. Destroyed station ≠ every practice or text erased.

## REMAINDER: unknowns, false bridges, contradictory evidence
Event's precise date, bishop/official roles, specific books/library status, affected priests, Dioscorus historical existence, connection to alchemical manuscripts.
