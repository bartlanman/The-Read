# ST-037 — Vespasian at Alexandria c.69–70 CE: A Miracle Claim as Political Legitimacy

**Event:** alleged miraculous healing in Alexandria during Flavian accession, usually c.69–70 CE.
**Witnesses:** Tacitus, `Histories` IV.81–82 (composed about 105–110 CE); Suetonius, `Vespasian` 7 (early second century). These are **later narratives**, not contemporary clinical observations.
**Status:** historical reception of *claimed* healing; **no validated miracle and no Alchemist station**.

## THERE
Vespasian needs political recognition after the 69 CE civil war. The Egyptian cult of Serapis offers a local divine language for legitimating sovereignty.

## CAPACITY AND CLAIM
A blind and a physically impaired person approach the emperor requesting cures; Tacitus says he consults physicians about whether recovery might be possible before complying. Later writers describe crowd acclaim and cures. These remain *reports* of extraordinary capacity.

## PERCEPTION AND AUTHORITY
Medical advisers, alleged divine instruction, the emperor and the observing public each play a distinct role in a narrative about whether the ruler has special capacity. The outcome is not independently tested through contemporary clinical records.

## SECRECY
No dangerous laboratory art or hidden formula is described. The claimed capacity is publicly demonstrated in the story, which is **an opposite publicity strategy to temple-hidden books**.

## LANGUAGE / ALLEGORY
Serapis-linked cure is an ancient religious-political account. Do not read it as a coded chemical transformation or as empirical proof of supernatural power.

## THE OUTSIDE
Political need for new emperor to gain legitimacy, local Serapis associations and a population seeking extraordinary signs.

## RESPONSE
The later narratives remember the claim as a public sign of authority. Differing authorial details caution against treating the witnesses as duplicate measurements of a medical event.

## HERE
**An institution or public can treat a witnessed claim as a source of authority without supplying independent evidence that the claimed causal mechanism worked.** This is distinct from craft appraisal and from pseudonymous recipe transmission.

## REMAINDER
Contemporary independent corroboration of patients and outcomes; exact dates, Tacitus's informants, roles of priests/physicians, whether stories developed in subsequent decades.
