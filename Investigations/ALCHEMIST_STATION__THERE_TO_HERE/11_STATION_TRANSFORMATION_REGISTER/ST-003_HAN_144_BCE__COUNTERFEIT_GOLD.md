# ST-003 — 144 BCE Han China: Law against counterfeit gold

**Scope:** Independent Chinese institutional/legal comparison; **not Greek-Egyptian alchemical lineage**.  
**Date:** 144 BCE (historical edict), Window 03 (160–141 BCE).  
**Witness:** *Hanshu* 5 compiled centuries afterward; subsequent annotations later still.  
**Verdict:** ATTESTED in retrospective dynastic history: legal ban on forged coins/gold; **NOT PROVEN** anti-alchemy law.

## THERE
Han state and monetary/material control; valuable gold and coinage used in economic exchange; technical crafts and ritual discourses existed within wider Chinese society.

## CAPACITY
A state law describes illicit fabrication; it does not describe a reproducible alchemical method or prove successful transmutation.

## PERCEPTION
Legal perspective: forged coins or gold are a serious offense. Later historiographic perspective: some scholars treated the law as aimed at alchemists.

## AUTHORITY
Imperial law determines which monetary acts are criminal, not who has attained the capacity to prepare elixirs.

## SECRECY AND THRESHOLD
A severe legal penalty might deter forgery. **Do not infer** that lawful alchemists became an underground class, that temple initiation was needed, or that a royal license was required for all ritual elixirs.

## LANGUAGE
Source: 定鑄錢偽黃金棄市律 (*Hanshu* 5). The operative object is currency and false/counterfeit gold. *Wei* 'counterfeit' is a legal adjective, not an alchemical code-name.

## THE OUTSIDE
Government pressure on monetary forgery and trust in gold. Different from a prohibition on material transformation for longevity, worship or theory.

## RESPONSE
Law was promulgated as remembered in the *Hanshu*; no contemporary record establishing an alchemist's distinct response has been identified.

## TEXT
*Hanshu* 5 records the law retrospectively. Later Ying Shao and Meng Kang glosses must not be cited as writings from 144 BCE.

## HERE
Historically meaningful state restriction over economic material objects. **It does not establish a prohibition of the station of Alchemist.**

## REMAINDER
Find contemporaneous legal manuscripts on enforcement; identify specific prosecuted craftspeople and whether any also practised elixir-making; distinguish alleged practice from technical evidence.

## HISTORIOGRAPHIC DISPUTE
Homer Dubs (1947) construed this as restriction of alchemy. Fabrizio Pregadio (2006) explains why it does not demonstrate a ban on Chinese alchemical elixirs. This is a disagreement about *interpretation of a law*, not evidence for an ancient school of secret masters.
