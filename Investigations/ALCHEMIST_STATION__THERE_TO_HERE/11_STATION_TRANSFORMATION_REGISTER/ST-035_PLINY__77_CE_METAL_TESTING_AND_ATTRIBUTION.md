# ST-035 — Pliny, c.77 CE: Testing Gold and Disputing the Authority of Names

**Region:** Rome and Roman imperial material culture.
**Event/text:** `Naturalis historia` about 77 CE, books 30 and 33.
**Witness:** Pliny is a **contemporary author** for the 61–80 CE interval, although manuscripts of his work are later.
**Status:** STRONG PERIOD EVIDENCE FOR material appraisal and dispute over inherited authorial prestige. **Not an Alchemist occupation.**

## THERE
Gold, silver, copper, dyes and jewels were marketable materials subject to imitation, gilding and expert evaluation. Literary accounts of magic claimed the expertise of venerable foreign and Greek masters.

## CAPACITY
Pliny NH 33 describes actual precious-metal decoration and gilding practices as well as a `coticula`, a touchstone used to judge metallic composition. **An applied gold surface is genuine gold material**, but the underlying base object need not be gold. Touchstone estimates are the author's claims about skilled appraisal, not validated modern assay precision.

## PERCEPTION
The object can look gold-colored and be presented as gold; specialist evaluation may reveal otherwise. A reader can also see a text attributed to Democritus while other authors doubt the attribution. Pliny in NH 30 **rejects the denial** in his account: his defense of Democritus's authenticity is not external proof.

## AUTHORITY
Goldsmiths, assessors, merchants and textual interpreters possess different authorities. Pliny uses compiler/judge authority to present these practices and discredit some magical claims. No professional guild or office literally titled 'Alchemist' is attested by these passages.

## SECRECY / LANGUAGE
`coticula` is a technical word for a touchstone, not a concealed ingredient. Osthanes and Democritus names supply magic's purported ancient pedigree. A claim of ancient authority is not a direct teacher–student lineage.

## THE OUTSIDE
Expensive metals and possible deception create economic pressure for tests. Claims of extraordinary magic face Roman elite scrutiny and competing readings of text attribution.

## RESPONSE
Pliny distinguishes surface finishing, fraudulent substitution and trained material examination, while criticizing magic (`fraudulentissima artium`) and discussing the antiquity of alleged masters.

## HERE
**A station of material judgment emerges separately from the craft station that produces objects—and independently from the authorial prestige used to defend a text.** Looking correct, being materially genuine and having authentic authorship are three different possible kinds of warrant.

## REMAINDER
Measured accuracy of ancient touchstone claims; specific names/credentials of appraisers; Pliny's exact texts on Democritus; relationship (if any) to Greek *Four Books*, unproven.
