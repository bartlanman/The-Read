# ST-027 — Liu Xin and the Seven Epitomes: The Bibliographic Station of Extraordinary Arts

**Date:** final Western Han, broadly 7–1 BCE, commonly c.5 BCE; do not assert an exact manuscript year.
**Place:** Western Han court.
**Witness:** Liu Xin's Qilüe is **lost**; classification transmitted and modified in the later first/second-century CE Hanshu Yiwenzhi.
**Status:** source-supported historical cataloging; **not validated alchemical technique**.

## THERE
Liu Xiang had been commissioned in 26 BCE to collate texts under Emperor Cheng. His son Liu Xin carried on the project. The father's earlier attempted production of gold in a royal workshop was unsuccessful; do not conflate that event with bibliographic competence.

## CAPACITY
Liu Xin organizes works by category. The later catalog preserves a class of fangji writings including medical canons (醫經), prescribed formulas (經方), bedchamber methods (房中) and transcendence/immortality arts (神仙).

## PERCEPTION AND AUTHORITY
State classification can give a type of knowledge an official bibliographic place, distinguish it from rival knowledge, preserve titles and shape later readers' evaluation.

## SECRECY AND LANGUAGE
Labels and archives are mechanisms of curation and access, not proof that every technical manuscript was deliberately encrypted. The lost original Qilüe should not be equated word for word with its Hanshu descendant.

## THE OUTSIDE AND RESPONSE
As knowledge collections became dispersed, a court-directed project retrieved and sorted works. Technical and longevity literature entered a durable classificatory tradition.

## HERE
**Institutional recognition of a body of writing does not validate its claims nor prove a practicing professional station.** An editor can govern access to knowledge without having mastered its practical execution.

## REMAINDER
Original Qilüe text, exact completion year, nature of particular lost works, editorial hierarchies and actual practitioners who followed its texts.
